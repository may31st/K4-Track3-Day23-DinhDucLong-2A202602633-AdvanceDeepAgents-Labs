# World models: from latent prediction to embodied control

## TL;DR

- A world model is best understood by its job: it represents observations and predicts how the environment may change, often conditional on an action, so an agent can plan or learn from imagined outcomes. Early latent-RL systems and newer video simulators implement that job differently. [1][2][3]
- Compact latent models can support efficient imagined control, while object-centric or hierarchical structure aims to preserve relations or timescales that a single state vector can blur. Their reported gains are tied to particular benchmarks, not a general ranking of architectures. [4][5][6]
- Generative video models make future visual states inspectable, and some work reports control utility, but high visual quality by itself does not establish accurate actions or physical executability. [3][7][8][9]
- Evaluation is shifting toward downstream planning and execution. Recent robotic studies report useful scaling and real-hardware results, but the evidence remains limited by task coverage, abstract-level reporting, and evaluation pipelines. [10][9][11]

## Background

“World model” does not name one settled architecture. In the model-based reinforcement-learning literature, it commonly means a learned representation of observations together with dynamics that predict future states under actions; models may also predict rewards or termination. The practical test is whether those predictions help an agent choose actions, not whether the system produces a complete scientific simulation of the world. [1][2]

The early World Models work made the latent approach concrete at a high level: the authors describe a compressed representation of spatial and temporal information, policy learning inside an imagined environment, and transfer of the resulting policy to the actual environment. The retrieved abstract supports a proof of concept for dream-based policy learning and transfer, rather than a claim of broad simulation accuracy. [1]

Dreamer developed a related architecture for model-based reinforcement learning. An observation representation, recurrent transition model and reward model provide latent rollouts; an actor and critic learn from these imagined trajectories instead of requiring every policy update to use a new environment interaction. The authors reported results across 20 visual-control tasks, but those comparisons establish performance only on the tested suite. [2] DreamerV3 continues this latent-imagination line and reports one configuration across more than 150 tasks, including Minecraft diamond collection from scratch. Its work emphasizes practical training choices such as normalization, loss balancing and reward transformations, so the reported breadth reflects a system and its engineering as well as its world model. [4]

One newer theoretical paper helps qualify what “good representation” means. It studies reinforcement learning under latent dynamics and gives conditions, including latent pushforward coverability, under which learning can be statistically tractable. These are theoretical claims about abstraction and function approximation, not results showing a particular neural model works in practice. [12] I use this survey’s term broadly for predictive systems used in planning or control, while keeping latent-RL models distinct from newer video-generation systems when comparing their evidence.

## Representation choices: compact latents, objects and timescales

A compact latent state can make imagined rollouts cheaper than generating every future pixel. Its usefulness depends on preserving features relevant to the decisions being made. Dreamer’s latent transition and reward predictions are designed to support policy improvement; they do not need to reproduce every visual detail if the compressed state is adequate for the evaluated control task. [2][4] The theoretical analysis reinforces a related caution: a latent representation is not automatically sufficient for tractable learning, and guarantees require assumptions about how the latent dynamics relate to observations and coverage. [12]

Object-centric models take a different route when relations between entities are important. SOLD proposes slot-based object representations and action-conditioned object dynamics for visual robotic manipulation. It combines imagined structured states with a transformer over slot histories for reward, value and action prediction. The abstract reports that SOLD outperforms DreamerV3 and TD-MPC2 on robotic environments requiring relational reasoning and manipulation. This comparison is bounded by the evaluated tasks and does not establish that object slots generally outperform holistic latents. [5]

A hierarchical latent model instead targets different temporal scales. H-JEPA proposes action-conditioned predictors at multiple levels, with higher levels predicting farther into the future and providing subgoals for lower levels. The authors report Visual AntMaze success increasing from 18% with a flat JEPA to 73% with a three-level hierarchy, alongside lower planner compute. Their real-robot-video result is offline planning fidelity on DROID, not a reported real-robot success rate. [6] Together, SOLD and H-JEPA show two specific ways to add structure: represent entities and their relations, or separate predictions by timescale. Their evaluations suggest useful inductive biases for selected tasks, but different environments and metrics prevent a direct comparison between the methods. [5][6]

## Video-generative models and hybrid designs

Generative video systems make predicted outcomes visible, which can help people inspect imagined futures and can supply training or planning data. Dynamic World Simulation adapts pretrained video generators with action-conditioned modules and a motion-reinforced objective. Its authors report action-controllable visual simulation and competitive model-based RL results, but the retrieved abstract does not supply enough numerical detail to compare those results fairly with latent-RL benchmarks. [3]

PAN combines latent autoregressive dynamics with a video diffusion decoder. In the described design, latent prediction models future states, while the decoder reconstructs detailed video conditioned on history and natural-language actions. The authors evaluate action-conditioned simulation, long-horizon forecasting and simulative reasoning, but the retrieved evidence does not include metric values that would establish how reliable the forecasts are over long horizons. [7] These approaches should not be treated as interchangeable with compact latent planners: rendered detail and action-conditioned prediction are capabilities to evaluate, while task utility requires a separate test.

Some recent work combines generative pretraining with compact action representations. ZimaBlue describes video pretraining, video-action mid-training and target-robot specialization, with a slower world-model representation and a faster action-prediction branch. The authors report that real-robot zero-shot success increased from 36.1% using target-robot data alone to 77.8% after scaling embodied-video pretraining to over 120,000 hours. This is a concrete author-reported result, but the retrieved abstract does not provide enough detail to attribute the change to video scale alone or to infer transfer across other robots and tasks. [13]

Enfold addresses inference cost by distilling future-generation computation into a representation computed from the present observation. Its authors report control experiments on LIBERO, RoboTwin2.0 and real-robot tasks, and report action latency reductions of 3.7 times relative to Fast-WAM and 10.1 times for Enfold-Flash. The retrieved abstract gives no task-by-task success values, and the latency comparison is with that named baseline rather than every generative model. I read these systems as evidence that a useful design space includes a rich generative training model and a faster deployed representation, rather than requiring the full video generator at each action step. [14]

## Evaluation: measure what predictions enable

The choice of metric can change which model appears useful. VP², a control-centric benchmark for video prediction, evaluates action-conditioned predictions through sampling-based planning in simulated manipulation tasks. Its authors report that common video metrics can be unreliable predictors of execution success and that better perceptual quality need not produce better planning. It covers 11 simulated task categories and 310 instances, so it gives direct evidence for its setting, not a universal verdict about video metrics. [8]

Newer benchmarks broaden the evaluation target. WorldArena evaluates 14 representative models across video perception, functional uses such as data generation, policy evaluation and action planning, and human assessment. It reports a gap between perception and functional utility; its planning component includes closed-loop action execution in RoboTwin simulation. This design makes downstream utility more visible than a single image score, though a simulated benchmark score still cannot establish real-world transfer. [10]

RoboWM-Bench examines a different link: whether predicted manipulation video can be turned into executable actions. The authors report a gap between visual realism and physical executability, lower success as task complexity rises, and continued physical inconsistencies even after manipulation-data fine-tuning. Their evaluation reconstructs scenes, converts predictions into actions and executes them in simulation. Pose retargeting, inverse dynamics and reconstruction all affect this pipeline, so the score tests a chain of components rather than video quality in isolation. [9]

The recent RoboJEPA abstract reports that its imagination error follows a second-order power law in compute and correlates strongly with downstream planning; it also describes zero-shot goal-image planning on real hardware. Those are promising claims for a predictive metric, but the HF Daily abstract does not provide numerical success rates, uncertainty estimates or task-level evaluation details. I would treat imagination error as a candidate proxy that needs independent, cross-task validation, rather than as a settled substitute for execution tests. [11]

## Trends and open problems

Across these papers, I see three practical shifts. First, authors increasingly judge a world model by the downstream use it supports, including planning, action execution, and policy learning, rather than only by the fidelity of generated images. [8][10][9] Second, architecture choices are becoming more explicit: object slots, temporal hierarchy, latent dynamics, video decoders and fast action branches each target different costs or task structure. [5][7][14][6] Third, researchers are beginning to report compute scaling and real-robot tests, although the retrieved evidence for these claims is often abstract-level and task-specific. [13][11]

Several gaps remain. Long-horizon predictions can look coherent while drifting in state or action consistency; the retrieved simulation papers describe these properties as goals, but do not establish reliable arbitrary-horizon physical prediction. [3][7][9] Offline prediction scores also need stronger validation against closed-loop task success across models, embodiments and environments. VP², WorldArena and RoboWM-Bench each make a case for functional evaluation, but their task suites and simulation or reconstruction pipelines limit what their results establish. [8][10][9]

Transfer remains a central issue. Results on simulated control, offline robot videos, or one real-hardware setup are not interchangeable. H-JEPA’s DROID result concerns offline planning fidelity, while ZimaBlue and RoboJEPA describe real-robot evaluation; each supports a different, limited claim. [13][6][11] More detailed reporting of seeds, task protocols, uncertainty, compute, action interfaces and failure cases would help readers compare results without treating unlike outcomes as one ranking. For now, I read world models as a family of methods whose value depends on the task, representation and evaluation loop, with no retrieved evidence here establishing a universally reliable model of the physical world. [4][12][10][9]
