# Efficient inference and small language models: a literature survey

## TL;DR

- Small language models are a design and training problem as much as a parameter-count problem. Recent work points to careful data mixtures and distillation from larger teachers as practical routes, each with distinct data and retraining requirements. [1][2][3]
- Inference gains depend on the workload. Paged KV memory, quantization, and speculative decoding can improve capacity or speed, while extra verification, memory traffic, and load can reduce or reverse the benefit. [4][5][6][7]
- Retrieval, sparse experts, and state-space models change where computation or memory is spent. They can help particular tasks or sequence regimes, but do not automatically make end-to-end inference cheaper. [8][9][10][11][12]
- Evaluation should report quality alongside latency, throughput, energy, and cost on the intended hardware. Recent SLM benchmarks and deployment studies expose gaps in measurement, including estimated rather than directly sensed energy and limited hardware coverage. [13][14][15][16]

## Background

“Small” is a deployment-relative label rather than a universal parameter threshold. A model that fits on one device may still be too slow at a long context, and an efficient model may consume more system resources once retrieval, batching, or a larger KV cache is included. An October 2024 survey organizes small-language-model work around architectures, training, compression, pruning, and quantization, while also emphasizing evaluation and open challenges. I use that taxonomy here, but treat model quality, runtime behavior, and device efficiency as connected questions rather than interchangeable measures. [1]

The field has two useful baselines. First, reducing a model’s parameter count can lower weight storage, but does not alone establish faster generation: memory bandwidth, attention state, precision support, and runtime kernels also matter. Second, inference is a sequence of prefill and token-generation operations with different bottlenecks. For example, PagedAttention manages the growing key-value (KV) cache in blocks, reducing wasted allocation and allowing larger batches. Its authors report 2 to 4 times higher serving throughput at similar latency than the systems they tested, while also reporting a 20 to 26 percent attention-kernel latency overhead. This is a useful warning that a local kernel cost and total serving performance need not move together. [4]

I interpret “efficient” broadly in this survey: fewer parameters or operations can matter, but so can achievable latency, concurrency, memory use, energy, and monetary cost. The evidence is heterogeneous. Some papers report benchmark maxima, some present a system or method, and some offer evaluation frameworks. Results below remain tied to their authors’ hardware, baselines, and tasks unless explicitly stated otherwise.

## Building capable models with less training and storage

Data quality and training design offer one route to a capable compact model. SmolLM2 is reported as a 1.7-billion-parameter model trained on about 11 trillion tokens, using staged mixtures of web, mathematics, code, and instruction data. Its authors introduce FineMath, Stack-Edu, and SmolTalk, and describe changing mixture rates based on performance from earlier stages. They report better results than Qwen2.5-1.5B and Llama3.2-1B on their evaluations. This shows how substantial data curation and staged training can support a capable small model; its final parameter count alone does not convey the scale of its training. [2]

A different strategy starts with a larger pretrained model and compresses it. Compact Language Models via Pruning and Knowledge Distillation combines pruning across depth, width, attention, and MLP components with teacher-guided retraining. For the Nemotron-4 family, the authors report 2 to 4 times compression. Their abstract also reports up to 40 times fewer training tokens in comparisons with Nemotron-3 8B and LLaMa2 7B. These are results from the paper’s tested family and comparison baselines; they should not be read as a comparison with training those variants from scratch. [3]

These examples give two different practical choices. Data-centric training builds a small model for the intended distribution, while pruning and distillation start from a pretrained teacher. The SmolLM2 study used about 11 trillion training tokens, whereas the pruning study reports compressing the Nemotron-4 family with pruning and distillation. The results reflect different design choices, not a head-to-head test of one efficiency measure. [2][3]

## Runtime techniques: memory, precision, and decoding

Serving systems must manage both model weights and the KV cache, which grows with context and concurrent requests. PagedAttention’s block allocation and cache sharing address allocation waste; its measurements show how a memory-management design can improve throughput even when one kernel is slower. The result is foundational, but it predates the recent-work window and comes from its own serving setup. I would not transfer its throughput ratio directly to a modern edge device or a small model without testing. [4]

Quantization lowers numerical precision to reduce storage and potentially speed computation, but the gain depends on hardware support and quality effects. Speculative decoding adds a draft-and-check process: a cheaper path proposes tokens and a more expensive path verifies them. QSpec combines low-precision drafting with higher-precision verification and reports up to 1.64 times speedup over its high-precision baseline without quality degradation, and up to 1.55 times over other speculative methods in batched settings. These are measured maxima in the authors’ evaluated configurations, not a general speedup guarantee. [5]

QuantSpec instead uses self-speculation with quantized weights and a hierarchical quantized KV cache. The authors report greater than 90 percent acceptance, up to about 2.5 times end-to-end speedup, and roughly a 1.3-fold reduction in memory requirements relative to the specific sparse-cache self-speculative alternatives they compare. The context is long-context inference; this evidence should not be read as an across-the-board comparison with all serving stacks. Together, QSpec and QuantSpec show that weight and cache precision can be co-designed with speculative verification, while their differing baselines make their headline speedups unsuitable for ranking one method above the other. [6]

Speculation can also become a workload-dependent scheduling choice. Nightjar reports that speculative decoding can increase throughput at low load when serving is memory-bound, but can hurt under high load when verification adds pressure to compute-bound GPUs. Its adaptive method changes speculative length or disables speculation as load changes, and the authors report up to 14.76 percent higher throughput and up to 20.18 percent lower latency than standard speculative decoding in their dynamic-arrival tests. The findings make concurrency part of the inference question: a technique that helps an isolated request may not help a crowded server. The reported gains remain benchmark-specific. [7]

## Architecture and conditional computation

Some approaches reduce the work performed per token by changing the model or moving knowledge outside its weights. Mixture-of-experts (MoE) models activate selected experts rather than all parameters for every token, but inactive experts still occupy memory and may need to be moved or loaded. eMoE predicts which experts a task will need and schedules their loading. Its authors report up to 80 percent lower memory use while maintaining accuracy and up to 17 percent lower inference latency against evaluated systems. The method illustrates why sparse operation counts are not sufficient for deployment planning: expert residency and loading can dominate, and task-aware prediction adds its own tradeoffs. [8]

Retrieval-augmented models store some knowledge in an external index. A 2024 study using a 1.4-trillion-token datastore reports quality gains on knowledge-intensive tasks and improved compute-optimal performance at the same training compute; smaller retrieval-based models sometimes outperform larger models without retrieval on those tasks. The authors also identify the inference costs: search and longer retrieved context. Benefits were weaker or mixed on reasoning-oriented benchmarks, so retrieval is better understood as a task-dependent allocation of knowledge than a universal substitute for model capacity. [9]

Runtime organization can reduce some of retrieval’s costs. FlashBack appends retrieved documents and marks that pattern during fine-tuning, in part to reuse the KV cache more effectively than iterative prepending. On its Llama 2 7B long-context test, the authors report up to four times faster inference than the prepending counterpart. This is a specific comparison rather than a general result for retrieval systems or small models, but it points to an important implementation issue: context arrangement affects cache reuse and runtime. [10]

State-space models (SSMs) offer another architecture choice. The original Mamba paper describes input-selective recurrent state updates and a hardware-aware scan, with autoregressive inference that does not keep a Transformer-style cache of prior elements; its authors report strong quality and throughput results against Transformers in their tested settings. Recent Mamba-3 work also recognizes that theoretically linear sequence models can face hardware-efficiency and state-tracking limits. At 1.5B parameters, its authors report 0.6 percentage points higher average downstream accuracy than the next-best Gated DeltaNet model, with a further 1.2-point gain for its MIMO variant, and comparable perplexity to Mamba-2 with half the state size. These results are promising for long sequences, but state size is not total device memory or a direct measure of energy, and the source does not establish a general runtime advantage across devices. [11][12]

## Measuring deployment efficiency

A model’s parameter count or theoretical operation count is an incomplete proxy for user-visible efficiency. The Watt Counts benchmark covers 50 LLMs across 10 NVIDIA GPUs in batch and server scenarios. It reports that suitable hardware choices vary by model and scenario. This broad LLM evidence includes SLM-relevant comparisons but is not an edge-accelerator study. [13]

SLM-Bench evaluates 15 small models on nine NLP tasks and 23 datasets across four hardware configurations, with measures spanning task correctness, computation, and resource consumption. It includes cost, estimated CO2 emissions, and energy, but explicitly omits runtime comparisons and uses standardized estimates rather than live energy-sensor readings. It is therefore useful for broadening what gets reported, while not serving as direct evidence about serving latency or measured real-time energy. [14]

Edge-oriented studies add device and service constraints. Edge-First Language Model Inference assesses models on individual edge devices and distributed edge clusters, and frames cloud fallback as necessary when device capacity or model quality is insufficient. A separate CPU, GPU, and NPU comparison reports that specialized accelerators can lead on performance, while low-power ARM systems may remain competitive on energy; its abstract also stresses bandwidth normalization and combined performance-energy metrics. These findings are bounded by tested devices and workloads, but they show why an SLM decision should name the target backend and fallback policy rather than assume that “runs on edge” means best overall. [15][16]

## Trends and open problems

The strongest common trend is co-design. Model compression, quantized arithmetic, cache layout, scheduling, and hardware cannot be assessed independently: PagedAttention’s kernel overhead alongside throughput gains and Nightjar’s load-sensitive speculation both demonstrate this at the system level. Reports should state batch size, prompt and output lengths, concurrency, latency percentiles, precision, memory use, quality, and hardware. Energy claims should clarify whether energy is directly measured or estimated. Current sources do not provide a single controlled comparison spanning these methods under matched models and workloads. [4][7][14]

There is also a distinction between making a compact model and making its inference efficient. SmolLM2’s 11-trillion-token training run and the Nemotron-4 compression results show that development routes can range from large-scale training to teacher-based compression. Conversely, a retrieval index, expert store, or cache optimization may save model-side computation while adding system memory, search, or scheduling overhead. Future studies should report both development cost and recurring inference cost, then test whether quality holds on domain-specific tasks and long-context workloads. [2][3][8][9]

For practical deployment, I would treat the model as one candidate component in a measured service, not infer its efficiency from size alone. Useful open comparisons include dense versus sparse models on the same accelerator, direct energy sensing across edge and server devices, and workload traces that capture changing load rather than fixed batches. The evidence surveyed here supports the value of these questions, but is not yet a unified cost-quality frontier: papers use different model families, baselines, hardware, and evaluation procedures. [13][14][15][16]
